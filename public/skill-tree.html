CS and AI Skill Tree

A rough picture of what a professional engineer in this area has in their head. The goal is really good fluency in CS and AI, not becoming an engineer. Finishing this map means you have the tools to do something real. It doesn't mean you're ready for real work, because getting good takes practice.

Simple projectYou can build it, with AI agents.
Medium projectYou can tell how it's made.
Really difficult projectYou understand how it's made, and can reasonably guess the tricky parts.

Roughly: simple is something one person can make for a small group. Medium is a real company product with its own engineering team. Really difficult runs at huge scale or carries a very hard promise. Sizes are one agent's judgment.

Do you need every block?

  • System design is the biggest lever. It's the one block marked as “do” for every medium and difficult product. Explaining how something is made is system design.
  • You need two depths. “Know” means you know what a block does and how it fails. “Do” means you can do it, with agents, and check the result. Your bar needs “know” for nearly everything and “do” for a few.
  • Where your own examples land (my placement). A learning or check-in app with accounts and progress is a simple Records product, and forthree is one. A pipeline over 100+ data sources is a Data platform, at roughly medium size. A local app is Software on your device. Real-time chat is a Live shared space. Traffic distribution, if you meant feeds and ranking, is Feeds or Search.

  • What held up without being asked. All four named AI as the biggest recent change, unprompted. Jobs and organizations also separated using AI tools, which spreads by employer more than by role, from building with models, which clusters in certain roles.

Four expectations to set

The bar is your three sizes, not “elite undergrad”

Top programs grade more and more on work done without AI, such as CS107, where coursework is now 20% of the grade. Matching that isn't your goal. Judging here means a first read: with basic information, you sense whether something will work and what to check. It doesn't mean expert critique. Each block has a worked example of that first read.

The automatic-car analogy needs one change

An automatic gearbox is reliable. AI isn't. So you're closer to a driving instructor with a second brake pedal. Typing and memorizing syntax are the manual gearbox, and you can drop them. Debugging, testing, specification and security are the second brake, and they matter more now. None I checked cited AI for cutting systems or theory, though Cal Poly did move theory to an elective.

Feeling like you learned it doesn't count

In METR's early-2025 study of 16 experienced developers, people took 19% longer with AI and believed they were about 20% faster. METR's late-2025 estimates point to speedups, though it calls that data unreliable. In Anthropic's trial of 52 mostly junior engineers, the AI group scored 50% on a quiz against 67% for hand-coders, with the biggest gap on debugging. That trial was small, tested right after the task, and came from an interested party. In the 2026 Stack Overflow survey, 71–73% of those answering said plausible but wrong code cost them time.

Scale: my guess is 800 to 1,700 hours for the whole map

That's ten trunk blocks at 60–120 hours each, plus 19 sub-areas at 10–25 hours each. No study has measured this. It comes to about 3 to 6.5 years at 5 hours a week, or about 1.5 to 3.5 years at 10. A small tool needs only parts of a few blocks, not all of these hours. For comparison, MIT's 6-3 major is about 2,500 nominal hours (177–183 units × 14). A goal of a small tool in 3 months is the first milestone, and forthree's design covers Code & debugging plus some vocabulary of later blocks.

How to learn with AI

My synthesis. No trial has tested these rules on adult self-learners, and evidence on AI tutors is mixed: a designed tutor helped in a Harvard study, unguarded GPT lowered exam scores in Turkish high schools, and a working paper found lower grades in one sample.

Attempt first

Write a prediction or an attempt before you ask AI. Stanford's CS106A and Berkeley's CS 61A both protect unaided practice early on.

Explain, then change

While learning a block, explain any generated code, then change it without AI. Real projects can lean on agents more.

Test later

Retest without AI 2 to 4 weeks later. A pass mark of about 80% on unseen items is my judgment.

Get graded

Use answer keys or public postmortems where they exist, and an engineer's quick reaction for the rest. Your own rating doesn't count.

Five driving principles

What carries over to any car. The list and its wording are my synthesis.

  • Abstractions leak. You can hand off the work, but you need to know what the abstraction hides.
  • A feel for cost. Estimate time, memory and money before you trust a result that works.
  • State, failure and trust boundaries. Lasting damage comes from shared state, partial failure and untrusted input.
  • Specify and verify. Rigor moves to saying what you want and showing it was done.
  • Own the mental model. You can outsource your thinking, but not your understanding (a line Karpathy quotes).

Where this map is most likely wrong

  • Whether understanding sticks without much doing. This assumption carries the most weight. No trial covers adult self-learners over months, which is why every block has something to build or explain.
  • Which items count as fluency. Evidence covers engineers and CS students, not experienced data scientists seeking fluency. A dropped item may matter more than the agents thought.
  • Your starting point. It is stated in your own words. Head starts rest on the word “basic”, so a block may go slower or faster than marked.
  • Vendor-heavy areas. AI apps and agents rely heavily on vendor write-ups, much of it from Anthropic, and the agents and I are Anthropic models. Read that area with that in mind.
  • Prompt framing. The neutral prompts still asked what changed most, so AI may have come up partly because of that.
  • What this evidence can't see. Postings show what employers ask, surveys show what people report, and curricula show what programs require. None of it tests what anyone knows.
  • One hardest job per product. Sorting products by a single promise is a simplification. Big products sit in three or four kinds, and the sort missed products whose trouble came from spikes or abuse.
