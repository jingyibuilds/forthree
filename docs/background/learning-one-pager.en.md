# Learning on One Page (v3, 2026-10-28)

English translation of [learning-one-pager.zh.md](learning-one-pager.zh.md), the original, which is in Chinese.

## 1. Definition

**Learning is a relatively lasting change in ability, caused by experience: after the scaffolding is removed, you can still do it, judge it, and apply it to a new situation on your own.**

As a process, it is the gradual replacement of **borrowed judgment** and **borrowed motivation** with **your own**.

## 2. Stages and types

**Process: linear in direction, not in path.**

Trigger → start → loop (each round ends in a small milestone; many rounds) → checkpoint (go deeper / go sideways / stop / reset the goal) → attainment → maintenance

- Linear: the overall move from not knowing to knowing; prerequisites in fields where each layer rests on the one below.
- Non-linear: round after round, plateaus, sudden jumps, turning back at checkpoints. Overall a spiral: the same thing returns after a while, each time deeper.

**Conscious ↔ unconscious: a continuum, alternating.**

- Leaning conscious: setting goals, deliberate practice, checking answers, checkpoints.
- Leaning unconscious: integration during idle time, automation once fluent, transfer you see at a glance, taste you cannot put into words.
- Rhythm: prepare consciously → integrate unconsciously → recall and test consciously.

**How far to go: decided per topic; stop when it is enough.**

- Depth: recognize → explain clearly → judge → produce
- Connection: scattered → joined into a whole → pushed into new fields
- Fluency: needs thought → needs none

## 3. Why make these distinctions

To know what support to give at each moment, so that an ordinary person stays in a healthy state of learning.

- **The most dangerous stretch: judgment runs ahead of ability.** You can tell good from bad but cannot yet make the good thing yourself. Motivation drops most easily here, and many people quit here (Ira Glass's "taste gap").
- **Strongly self-driven people get through on motivation, and good self-learners find their own checkpoints. Ordinary people lack both.** So the system lends first: judgment (direction, evidence, feedback on mistakes) and motivation (rhythm, small milestones, peers, deadlines). Then it withdraws them step by step.
- Research behind this: external motivation can be internalized step by step into one's own (the internalization continuum of self-determination theory); novices need strong guidance, and experts need it removed (the expertise reversal effect).

## 4. A general design framework: fits any topic

1. **Fix the endpoint and the evidence first.** Rewrite "I want to learn X" as a task that can be checked. Set a level for each topic; stop when it is enough.
2. **Start from the learner's starting point.** Test what they already know and what transfers; do not re-teach it. Map the field from several sources, and choose the way of learning from the field's structure.
3. **In the loop, let the learner try first. Mistakes are first-class material.** Keep difficulty so that most answers are right and a few are wrong. A mistake marks the edge of ability and is also the best practice.
4. **Milestones must be dense, and real.** Every so often, remove the scaffolding and test what the learner can do without hints. Tie rewards to ability, not to activity.
5. **Lend motivation first, then withdraw it.** Rhythm, reminders, peers and deadlines are lent out; balance rewards against urgency. Once ability is visible, swap them for goals and a sense of achievement the learner chose.
6. **Watch the signals, and call the checkpoint early.** Skipping, plateaus, an error rate that will not fall, dropping motivation: change the goal, change the form, add peers. Do not wait for the learner to quit.
7. **The place of AI.** Set questions, give feedback, ask follow-ups, summarize, argue the other side. It does not make the attempts for the learner, and it does not simply agree.
8. **Judge attainment on a real task, then maintain.** Can they do it in a situation they have not seen? Recall at low frequency, refresh what changes, and allow what is no longer needed to be set down.

## Case: the framework applied to Duolingo

| What it does well | What it does not do |
|---|---|
| A very low barrier to start, a fixed daily rhythm, dense small achievements | Rewards are tied to activity (XP, streaks), not to ability |
| Spaced repetition backed by an algorithm (half-life regression; daily active users +12% after launch) | Motivation stays borrowed; withdrawing it was never designed |
| Lends you motivation: streaks, leaderboards, reminders | Practice is mostly recognizing and choosing, so little is produced with scaffolding removed; no independent evidence for speaking |
| Reading and listening can reach the level of the fourth college semester (Jiang et al. 2021; the authors include Duolingo researchers) | Little attention to starting point and transfer (the author's experience: a native Chinese speaker learning Japanese still starts from kanji) |
| | The endpoint is not a real task, and there is no re-deciding at checkpoints |

**One-line diagnosis**: Duolingo took lending motivation to the extreme, but lent judgment only at the level of "recognize". It never designed the step of removing the scaffolding, so motivation stops at the check-in and ability stops at recognition.
